NASA Glenn Research Center

Rocket Propulsion

Thrust is the force which moves a rocket through the air and through space. It is generated by the propulsion system through the application of Newton's third law of motion: for every action there is an equal and opposite re-action.

Propellant: fuel and oxidizer

Unlike a jet engine, a rocket carries its own oxidizer — liquid oxygen, for example — so it can burn its fuel where there is no air, including the vacuum of space.

Combustion chamber

Fuel and oxidizer are mixed and burned in the combustion chamber, producing hot exhaust gas at very high pressure.

The nozzle

The converging–diverging nozzle accelerates the exhaust to supersonic speed. The faster the exhaust leaves, the more thrust the engine makes for the propellant it burns.

Liquid and solid rockets

Liquid engines can be throttled and restarted; solid rocket motors are simpler but burn until the propellant is gone.

Specific impulse

Engineers compare engines by specific impulse — how much thrust an engine gets from each kilogram of propellant per second. Higher exhaust velocity means higher specific impulse and a more efficient engine.

Staging

Because an engine has to lift its own empty tanks, large rockets drop spent stages and light new engines on the way up, so less dead weight is carried to orbit.
